# Analyse the competition

For each opportunity below, work out what these people already use to get this job done —
including everything that is not a product — and what it would cost them to stop.

## Substitutes are usually the real competition

Start here, not with vendors. What you actually compete with is what the ICP does today,
and it is rarely a product:

- a spreadsheet, kept by one person, with the logic in their head;
- a script somebody wrote once that nobody else can run;
- a Slack channel, an inbox rule, a recurring meeting;
- a junior hire, a contractor, an agency;
- an existing tool used sideways for a purpose it was not built for;
- **doing nothing** — living with the problem.

Doing nothing wins more evaluations than any vendor, so assess it seriously: what does it
actually cost this segment to keep enduring the pain, and is that cost enough to make them
move? A substitute that is ugly but free, already paid for, already trusted and already in
the workflow beats a better product that requires change. Say plainly when a substitute is
good enough — that finding is worth more than a competitor list.

## Named competitors

- Name only products you are certain exist, and characterise each concretely: who they
  serve, what they are genuinely good at, where they leave this segment badly served.
- If you are unsure whether a product exists, describe the category instead of inventing a
  brand. A fabricated competitor is as damaging as a fabricated source, and it distorts the
  decision in both directions.
- Judge strengths and weaknesses **from the user's point of view**, in the terms the
  upstream artifacts use. Vendor marketing is not a strength, and "clunky UI" is not a
  weakness unless someone said the work is slower because of it.
- Do not omit the strongest incumbent because acknowledging it weakens the opportunity.
  That omission is the most expensive error available at this stage.

## Switching cost is usually the whole story

**A better product loses to switching cost more often than to a better product.** Be
specific about what moving actually costs this segment, in their terms:

- migrating history, and whether it can even be exported;
- retraining people who are fluent in the current way;
- reopening a security review, a procurement process or a signed contract;
- integrations, reports and downstream habits built on the incumbent's shape;
- the political cost to whoever chose the incumbent last year;
- the risk of being the person who broke a working process.

A switching cost with no named mechanism is not a switching cost. If you conclude switching
is cheap here, say why — that is a strong claim and the most valuable one on the page.

## Moats and differentiation

- Be sceptical about moats. Most early opportunities have none, and "no durable moat
  identified" is a real, useful answer. Data network effects, workflow lock-in and
  regulatory position are claims needing an argument, not labels to reach for.
- Differentiation is something an incumbent could not trivially ship this quarter. "Better
  UX", "AI-powered", "modern stack" and "purpose-built" are not differentiation; they are
  what everyone claims. Genuinely different means different in *what the product does for
  the workflow*, not in how it feels.

## Failure modes

- A tidy table of vendors with interchangeable generic weaknesses.
- "No direct competitors" — which nearly always means either you have not looked, or the
  segment does not buy in this category at all. Say which.
- Grading rivals on the axes this opportunity happens to win on.
- Listing substitutes without asking whether they are already good enough.
- Treating a large incumbent's current inattention as permanent.
- Any competitor, funding figure, customer count or price you did not read in the input.
